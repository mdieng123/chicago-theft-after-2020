Write-up

The data

We use the City of Chicago's Crimes – 2001 to Present dataset, which the Chicago Police Department publishes on the Chicago Data Portal. Each record is one reported crime, with its date and time, crime type, a more specific description, the kind of place it happened, whether an arrest was made, and the community area.

We kept the two theft categories, THEFT and MOTOR VEHICLE THEFT, for the ten full years 2016–2025, which gives records. We included motor vehicle theft because the dataset files it separately from theft, and leaving it out would hide a large part of the post-2020 change. 2026 is left out because it is a partial year. We compare three periods: 2016–2019 as the baseline, 2020 as the pandemic year (shown but not counted in either average), and 2021–2025 as "after".

The full dataset has more than 8 million rows, and the portal's export timed out, so we pulled only the rows we needed through the portal's API (scripts/download_raw.sh, data retrieved October 13, 2026). A Python script (scripts/prepare_data.py) then cleaned and aggregated them:

  • Theft type. About 30 description values were grouped into 8 types (retail, from a building, pickpocket/purse snatching, over $500, $500 and under, other theft, automobile, other vehicle). Attempted thefts are counted with their type.
  • Location type. About 140 location values were grouped into 8 kinds of place. Spellings change across years (for example RESIDENCE-GARAGE and RESIDENCE - GARAGE), so we matched on keywords, using whole-word matches so that, for example, "barbershop" is not counted as a bar.
  • Side of the city. Each of the 77 community areas was mapped to one of Chicago's 9 "sides". A handful of records with no community area are labeled "Unknown".
  • Aggregation. The records were summed into counts by month and by hour of day for every combination of year, theft type, location type and side, which keeps the files the page loads under 3 MB.

Limitations. These are reported crimes, so changes can reflect reporting as well as crime. About 3% of thefts are logged at exactly 12:00 AM, usually because the true time is unknown (often thefts discovered later), so the midnight hour is overstated. The portal omits the most recent seven days and can revise records, so counts may differ slightly from a later download.

Design rationale

View 1: thefts per month

A line chart is the clearest way to show volume over time, and plotting months rather than years keeps both the sharp drop in spring 2020 and the seasonal summer peaks visible. Shaded bands mark the three periods so the reader can compare before and after at a glance, a dashed line marks the March 2020 stay-at-home order, and a dashed horizontal line shows the 2016–2019 monthly average as a fixed baseline. The baseline ignores the year filter, so it stays put when the reader zooms in. Hovering snaps to the nearest month and shows the count, its share of the current selection, its change from the baseline and the arrest rate. Dragging across the chart sets the year range for every view, which makes this chart the overview for the whole page.

Alternatives considered: yearly bars were simpler but hid the 2020 drop and the seasonality; a stacked area chart by theft type showed composition but made the total and the baseline hard to read, and View 2 already covers which types changed; small multiples by side gave nine small charts that were hard to compare, so side became a filter instead.
