Write-up

About the data

We used the Crimes - 2001 to Present dataset from the Chicago Data Portal. Each row is one reported crime and has the date and time, the type of crime, a description, the type of place, whether someone was arrested, and the community area.

We only kept THEFT and MOTOR VEHICLE THEFT from 2016 to 2025, which is 716,105 rows. We included motor vehicle theft because it is a separate category in the data and it changed the most after 2020. We left out 2026 because the year isn't over. We compare 2016-2019 (before) to 2021-2025 (after) and show 2020 on its own because of the pandemic.

The full dataset is over 8 million rows and the export kept failing, so we downloaded only the rows we needed using the portal's API. Then we used a Python script to group the data:

  • Theft type: 8 groups (retail, from building, pickpocket/purse, over $500, $500 and under, other theft, automobile, other vehicle).
  • Location type: 8 groups (street/alley, residence, retail store, restaurant/bar, transit, parking, commercial/office, other). The same place is spelled differently in different years, so we matched on keywords.
  • Side of the city: the 77 community areas grouped into Chicago's 9 sides.

Then we counted thefts by month and by hour so the files are small enough to load in the browser.

Limitations: this is only crime that was reported. Also, about 3% of thefts are recorded at exactly 12:00 AM, usually because no one knows the real time, so midnight looks higher than it really is.

Design decisions

View 1: thefts per month

We used a line chart because it's the clearest way to show change over time. We used months instead of years so you can see the big drop in spring 2020 and the summer peaks every year. The background is shaded for before, 2020 and after. Dashed lines show the stay-at-home order (March 2020) and the 2016-2019 monthly average, so you can tell if a month was above or below normal. The average line stays the same when you zoom in, so you're always comparing to the same baseline.

Interactions:

  • Tooltip (details on demand): hover to see the number of thefts that month, its share of the selection, how it compares to the 2016-2019 average, and the arrest rate.
  • Brushing and linking: drag across the chart to zoom every chart to those years. Reset zooms back out.

Other options we thought about: a bar chart by year was simpler but hid the 2020 drop and the seasons. A stacked area chart by theft type made the total hard to read, and View 2 already shows the theft types.

AI usage

We used AI (Claude) as a coding helper. The design decisions were ours: we picked the dataset and our question, decided which crimes and years to include, chose the theft type and location groups, and designed the three linked views and how you interact with them. AI helped us turn those decisions into code faster.

  • Person A: used AI to write the script that downloads the data through the API (after the portal's export kept failing) and the cleaning script, to turn our View 1 design into D3 code, and to help fix bugs. We checked its work by comparing our row counts to the portal's yearly totals, printing every location value to make sure it landed in the right group, and testing the filters in the browser.
  • Person B: to fill in.
  • Person C: to fill in.

AI saved a lot of time, but we still had to catch its mistakes. For example, simple keyword matching put barbershops in the "bar" group and bowling alleys in "street/alley", and the first version of the code was more complicated than it needed to be, so we rewrote it in the same style as our labs.
